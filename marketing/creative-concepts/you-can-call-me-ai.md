# Tagline: "Halbert. You can call me AI."

## Concept

**Primary tagline:**

> Halbert. You can call me AI.

## Why it works

Three layers of wordplay converge on a single five-word line:

1. **HAL 9000 allusion** — The name "Halbert" reads as Hal + Albert. "Hal" evokes HAL 9000 from *2001: A Space Odyssey*, the iconic AI. The connection is implicit; the audience supplies it themselves.

2. **Paul Simon callback** — "You can call me Al" is the title of a well-known Paul Simon song. Swapping "Al" for "AI" turns a familiar phrase into a product promise: this is an AI you're on a first-name basis with.

3. **Al / AI visual pun** — In many typefaces, lowercase "al" and "ai" are nearly or exactly identical. The eye reads one thing, the mind reads another. This is the strongest hook in the line and the most original element.

The cumulative effect: a tagline that feels personal ("call me"), references the AI heritage without naming it, and rewards a second look.

## Legal considerations

Not legal advice. Summary of the risk landscape as of 2026-08-23.

### 2001: A Space Odyssey / HAL 9000 (Warner Bros. / MGM)

- **Low risk as written.** The tagline contains no direct reference to HAL 9000, 2001, Kubrick, MGM, or Warner Bros. No mark is used; no copyrighted expression is reproduced.
- "Halbert" is a real surname and a plausible portmanteau of Hal + Albert. It is not "HAL" or "HAL 9000," which are the registered trademarks.
- The connection is an **allusion**, not a use of the mark. Allusion is not trademark infringement. Trademark protects against consumer confusion about source, and no reasonable consumer would believe Warner Bros. produced this app.
- **Where risk would increase:** pairing the tagline with visual trade dress that directly evokes the film — the red glowing camera eye, "I'm sorry, Dave, I'm afraid I can't do that," the monolith, the Strauss waltz. Those are protectable expression. Keep the copy; skip the red eye.

### Paul Simon / "You Can Call Me Al"

- Song titles and short common phrases of this form are not protectable in the way that would trigger concern.
- Usage is transformative wordplay, not the song itself.
- Lowest-risk element of the concept.

### Al / AI visual pun

- Original wordplay. No IP issue.

### General caveat

"Low risk" is not "zero risk." Studios have litigation budgets and occasionally send C&Ds on thin pretexts. If this scales into a funded company with real marketing spend, a brief consult with a trademark attorney is worth the peace of mind. For an early-stage project, the line is safe to run as-is.

## Usage notes

- The visual pun depends on typeface choice. Test in the intended font before committing to layouts. Sans-serif humanist fonts (where lowercase "a" is a single-story form and "i" / "l" are narrow) tend to sell the pun best.
- Pair with a wordmark, not a logomark, so the "AI" reading is reinforced by context.
- Avoid any visual reference to a red glowing lens, a monolith, or space-station imagery. The allusion should live in the name and the ear, not the art.
